The Code of the Quad

Tagging. Crews alternate dropping a tag of their own color. Graphite goes first. A tag is only legal if it has at least one non-repeat distance ("beef") to an existing same-color tag — you can't set yourself up for a repeat on purpose.

Calling out beef. Once you tag, the rival crew picks one of your existing tags and measures the distance to your new one. That distance is now on record for your color — forever.

The box-in. If every open square on the blacktop would force a repeat for the crew to move, they're boxed in — no legal tag left anywhere. By default the boxed-in crew gets busted (standard "no legal move" rule); flip the house rule above if you'd rather reward getting cornered.

Turf meters. The bars under the blacktop show how many legal tags each crew has left, live. Watch them drain. When the rival crew calls out your beef, they're not just picking a number — they're deciding how much of your future turf just got burned. Big burns land a BIG HIT.

Draws. If the blacktop fills up completely with only legal tags, it's a truce — rare, since a box-in usually ends things first.

NPC difficulty, weakest to strongest: Freshman → Sophomore → Junior → Senior → Valedictorian, ranked over thousands of simulated turf wars.

Why clustering wins. Short distances near a cluster come from a tiny palette (1, 2, 4, 5, 8…), so packing tags together burns through that palette fast — which is what tends to box in the rival's next tag first. Valedictorian combines that with a 1-move lookahead: it tags wherever leaves the rival crew the fewest legal squares next turn, and among ties, packs in tight.

On balance. With "get busted" as the house rule and two Valedictorian NPCs, a 16×16 blacktop comes out to roughly 51%/49% — about as fair a turf war as this game gets.
