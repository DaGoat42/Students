Rules & how the bots think

Placing. Players alternate placing a stone of their own color. Black moves first. A placement is only legal if the new stone has at least one non-repeat distance to an existing same-color stone — you can't voluntarily set up a repeat.

Pairing. Once placed, the other player picks one existing same-color stone to pair it with (when more than one legal option exists). That distance gets marked "used" for that color, forever.

The trap. If every empty cell on the board would force a repeat for the player to move, they're trapped — there's no legal square left. By default the trapped player loses (the standard "no legal move" convention); flip the house rule above if you'd rather reward being cornered.

Draws. If the board fills up completely with only legal placements, it's a draw — rare, since traps usually end the game well before that.

Bot strategies, weakest to strongest: Highest avg. distance → Random → Worst strategy → Lowest avg. distance → Best strategy, measured over thousands of simulated games.

Why clustering is strong. Short distances near a cluster come from a tiny palette (1, 2, 4, 5, 8…), so packing stones together burns through that palette fast — which is what tends to trap the opponent's next placement first.

Best strategy combines that with a 1-ply lookahead: it picks the placement that leaves the opponent the fewest legal squares next turn, and among ties, prefers the tightest cluster. Worst does the opposite on purpose.

On balance. With the "trapped player loses" house rule and two Best-strategy bots, a 16×16 board comes out to roughly 51%/49% — about as fair as this game gets between the two colors.
