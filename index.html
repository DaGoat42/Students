How to Play

Plotting a point. You alternate placing a point of your own color. Pencil goes first. A point is only legal if it has at least one non-repeat distance to an existing same-color point — you can't set yourself up for a repeat on purpose.

Measuring up. Once you plot a point, your rival picks one of your existing points and measures the distance to your new one. That distance is now on record for your color — forever.

Getting cornered. If every open square on the notebook page would force a repeat for whoever's turn it is, they're stuck — no legal point left anywhere. By default the stuck student loses (standard "no legal move" rule); flip the house rule above if you'd rather reward getting cornered.

Move meters. The bars under the page show how many legal moves each of you has left, live. Watch them drain. When your rival measures your point, they're not just picking a number — they're deciding how much of your future room to move just got cut off. Big cuts land a BIG SQUEEZE.

Draws. If the board fills up completely with only legal points, it's a draw — rare, since getting cornered usually ends things first.

NPC difficulty, weakest to strongest: Freshman → Sophomore → Junior → Senior → Valedictorian, ranked over thousands of simulated matches.

Why clustering wins. Short distances near a cluster come from a tiny palette (1, 2, 4, 5, 8…), so packing points together burns through that palette fast — which is what tends to corner your rival's next point first. Valedictorian combines that with a 1-move lookahead: it plots wherever leaves the rival the fewest legal squares next turn, and among ties, packs in tight.

On balance. With "loses" as the house rule and two Valedictorian NPCs, a 16×16 board comes out to roughly 51%/49% — about as fair a match as this game gets.
